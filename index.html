BARCELONA — The Modernista City

Barcelona is the capital of Catalonia, a region with its own language, its own history, and a fierce sense of itself as distinct from the rest of Spain. You'll hear Catalan as often as Spanish. The city you're visiting was largely shaped by two forces: a medieval maritime empire that made it rich, and a late-19th-century industrial boom that made it modern — and gave it Modernisme, the Catalan strand of Art Nouveau that produced Antoni Gaudí.

Sagrada Família

Gaudí's basilica is the most-visited monument in Spain and, improbably, still a construction site. Work began in 1882; Gaudí took over a year later and spent the last 43 years of his life on it, living on-site as an increasingly devout recluse until a tram killed him in 1926. He knew he'd never see it finished — his answer was that "my client is not in a hurry."

What makes it extraordinary isn't just the scale but the logic. Gaudí distrusted straight lines ("the straight line belongs to men, the curved line to God") and built the entire structure from forms found in nature — the columns branch like trees to hold the roof, the towers rise like termite mounds, the light through the stained glass is tuned so the nave glows green-blue (the sea, morning) on one side and red-orange (fire, evening) on the other. He worked out the impossible geometry using upside-down models weighted with little bags of birdshot — hanging chains that, inverted, gave him the perfect load-bearing arches. He was doing by string and gravity what engineers now do by computer. Point this out to the boys: this is physics you can see.

Three façades tell the story of Christ — the Nativity façade (the one Gaudí completed, dense and joyful) and the Passion façade (stark, angular, built after his death and deliberately harsh) face different directions, so the late-afternoon light on the Passion side is the dramatic one. When complete, the central Tower of Jesus Christ will make it the tallest church in the world.

Passeig de Gràcia & the Block of Discord

Your apartment sits on Barcelona's grandest boulevard, and it's an open-air museum of Modernisme. One block — nicknamed the Illa de la Discòrdia ("Block of Discord") — holds competing masterpieces by the three great Modernista architects, built side by side for rival bourgeois families showing off their new industrial money. The joke was that the buildings "argued" with each other in style.

Casa Batlló

Gaudí's remodel of an ordinary apartment block into something that looks alive. The local nickname is the House of Bones — the balconies read as skulls, the pillars as femurs. The real theme is the legend of Saint George (Sant Jordi), patron of Catalonia, slaying the dragon: the scaled, iridescent roof is the dragon's back, the turret is George's lance driven into its spine. There isn't a straight wall or right angle in the place; the light-well is tiled darker blue at the top and paler below so the light distributes evenly all the way down. It's air-conditioned throughout — a mercy in July, and one reason late afternoon is a fine time to visit.

La Sagrada Família

Gaudí's unfinished cathedral, begun in 1882 and still rising — he took it over at 31 and worked on it for 43 years, the last dozen living on the site. Told it would take centuries, he shrugged: "My client is not in a hurry." He was killed by a tram in 1926, mistaken for a beggar because of his shabby clothes. The building is an argument in stone that nature and geometry are the same thing: columns branch like trees to carry the vaults, and the whole structure was worked out with hanging chain models — inverted, they trace the exact curves that stand in pure compression. Two façades tell the story from opposite temperaments: the Nativity side (built in Gaudí's lifetime) teems with carved life, while the stark Passion side is all bone and angle. Inside is the real surprise — a forest of light, with sunrise-side windows in cool blues and greens, sunset-side in reds and golds, so the colors migrate across the nave through the day. Your late-afternoon slot catches the warm end.

The Gothic Quarter & El Born (your day with Patrick)

This is the medieval and Roman heart of the city — narrow stone lanes, hidden squares, the 14th-century cathedral, and fragments of the Roman wall from when this was Barcino. El Born, next door, was the medieval merchants' district and is now the most atmospheric part of the old city. A good local guide (which you have) turns a maze into a story: guild streets still named for the trades that worked them, the church of Santa Maria del Mar built by the port workers themselves, the scars of the 1714 siege that ended Catalan independence. Let Patrick lead; the pleasure here is in being walked through it.

ROME — The Eternal City

Rome is 2,800 years of continuous city stacked on top of itself: the ancient imperial capital, the seat of the Catholic Church, and a working modern metropolis, all occupying the same few square miles. The layering is literal — churches built on temples built on houses. Your days move roughly chronologically: ancient Rome, then the Christian and Baroque city.

Your ETuk Orientation Loop (Saturday evening)

Your first evening is a private electric tuk-tuk glide past Rome's greatest hits — a painless way to get your bearings before you dive into the big sites over the coming days. Highlights along the ~30 km route: the Colosseum; the Circus Maximus, the vast grassy hollow that was antiquity's ~250,000-seat chariot-racing stadium; the Aventine's Orange Garden (Giardino degli Aranci), a serene terrace with one of the city's best skyline views; the Bocca della Verità, the ancient marble "Mouth of Truth" said to bite the hands of liars; Teatro di Marcello, a theatre begun by Julius Caesar and finished by Augustus (~13 BC) that predates the Colosseum by a century and was its model — with Renaissance apartments built right onto its ancient top that are still lived in today; the Jewish Ghetto with the Portico d'Ottavia and Turtle Fountain; Piazza Navona, built over Domitian's stadium and centered on Bernini's Four Rivers Fountain; the Pantheon; Piazza di Pietra, where the columns of Hadrian's Temple are embedded in a later palazzo; the Trevi Fountain and Spanish Steps; the Mausoleum of Augustus, the round tomb of Rome's first emperor; St. Peter's Square; the Gianicolo (Janiculum) panorama and the monumental Fontana dell'Acqua Paola; and Trastevere, the atmospheric tangle of medieval lanes across the Tiber. You'll see several of these properly later in the week — think of tonight as the trailer.

The Colosseum & Roman Forum

The Colosseum (properly the Flavian Amphitheatre) opened in 80 AD and held 50,000–80,000 spectators, seated strictly by rank, moved through 80 numbered arches by a ticketing system Roman engineers would recognize in any modern stadium. Beneath the wooden arena floor ran the hypogeum — a two-level warren of cages, ramps, and elevators that lifted animals and scenery up through trap doors. Gladiators were the professional athletes of their day: mostly enslaved, occasionally free volunteers, and valuable enough that most fights did not end in death. If your tour includes standing on the arena floor, that's the moment worth the money — you're where the sand ("arena" means sand, laid down to absorb blood) once was, looking up at the tiers.

The Roman Forum next door was the civic center of the ancient world — law courts, temples, the Senate house, the spot where Caesar's body was burned. It reads today as a field of ruins, which is exactly why a guide matters: they rebuild it for you in words.

The Pantheon

The best-preserved building of ancient Rome, and the one that most rewards simply standing inside it. Built by Hadrian around 125 AD on the site of an earlier temple by Marcus Agrippa — whose name still crowns the portico (M·AGRIPPA·L·F·COS·TERTIVM·FECIT) — it was a temple to all the gods: pan-theon. Its unreinforced concrete dome — 43.3 meters across, and exactly as tall as it is wide, so a perfect sphere would fit inside — remained the largest in the world for over 1,300 years and is still the largest of its kind ever built. The coffered ceiling steps up to the oculus, the 9-meter open eye at the summit and the only light source: a slow disc of sun that tracks across the walls by day, open to the sky, so rain falls straight through and drains away through weep-holes in the gently sloped marble floor. It survived the fall of Rome because in 609 AD it was consecrated as a church, Santa Maria ad Martyres — which it remains.

Beneath that ancient dome now rest some of Italy's illustrious dead:

  • Raphael (1483–1520), the great High Renaissance painter, who asked to be buried here; his Latin epitaph, composed by Cardinal Pietro Bembo (whom you'll have just seen entombed at the Minerva), says that Nature feared to be outdone by him while he lived, and to die when he died. His fiancée, Maria Bibbiena, lies beside him.
  • Vittorio Emanuele II (d. 1878), the first king of a united Italy, honored here as Padre della Patria — "Father of the Fatherland."
  • Umberto I (d. 1900), the second king, assassinated at Monza — and his wife Queen Margherita, for whom the pizza Margherita was named.

Santa Maria sopra Minerva

Two minutes behind the Pantheon stands the one place in Rome where the Middle Ages survived intact: the city's only true Gothic church interior, its vaults painted a deep midnight blue and scattered with gold stars. The Dominicans raised it in the 13th century atop the ruins of an ancient temple long thought to be Minerva's — hence sopra Minerva, "over Minerva." Out in the little piazza, Bernini's beloved baby elephant carries an Egyptian obelisk on its back — his witty emblem that a strong mind is needed to support solid wisdom.

Inside are two masterpieces you can walk right up to. To the left of the high altar stands Michelangelo's Risen Christ (1521), a serene, muscular nude Christ embracing the cross (the bronze loincloth was a later, more bashful addition). In the right transept, the Carafa Chapel glows with Filippino Lippi's frescoes (1488–93) — a soaring Annunciation and Triumph of St. Thomas Aquinas among the freshest paintings of the early Renaissance.

Above all, the Minerva is a great resting place of the famous. Beneath the high altar lies St. Catherine of Siena (d. 1380), Doctor of the Church and co-patron of Europe — her body here, her head enshrined back in Siena. Close by rests the painter-friar Fra Angelico ("Beato Angelico," d. 1455), so gentle a soul he was named patron saint of artists. And the church holds five popes, more than almost any in the city:

  • Leo X and Clement VII — the Medici cousins, in grand tombs behind the altar. Leo excommunicated Luther and bankrupted the treasury building the new St. Peter's; Clement lived through the 1527 Sack of Rome and refused Henry VIII his divorce, breaking England from Rome.
  • Paul IV (in the Carafa Chapel) — the iron pope of the Inquisition (the Church's tribunal for hunting down heresy), the Index of Forbidden Books (its official list of banned reading), and the Roman Ghetto (the walled, nightly-locked quarter into which he forced the city's Jews) — so loathed that the mob toppled and beheaded his statue when he died in 1559.
  • Urban VII (5th chapel, right) — your "twelve-day pope," the shortest reign in history: elected 15 September 1590, he died of malaria twelve days later, before he could even be crowned; his monument was funded by a charity he'd made his heir, which dowered poor girls for marriage.
  • Benedict XIII (d. 1730) — a Dominican, fittingly buried in his order's Roman church.

In the floor beneath Leo X lies the humanist Cardinal Pietro Bembo, friend of Michelangelo and Raphael, who wrote Raphael's epitaph over in the Pantheon. And one last thread of history hangs here: in the Dominican convent attached to the church, Galileo was tried by the Inquisition in 1633 and forced, on his knees, to recant the idea that the Earth moves.

The Vatican: Museums, Raphael Rooms & the Sistine Chapel

The Vatican is the world's smallest sovereign state and the largest art collection on Earth. You pass through the Museums toward two climaxes. First the Raphael Rooms, whose School of Athens is the great picture of the Renaissance mind — Plato and Aristotle at the center, every philosopher of antiquity given the face of one of Raphael's contemporaries (Plato is Leonardo; the brooding figure on the steps is Michelangelo).

Then the Sistine Chapel. Michelangelo, a sculptor who insisted he was no painter, spent four years (1508–1512) on his back on scaffolding to cover the ceiling with the Creation — the near-touching fingers of God and Adam being the most reproduced image in Western art. He returned decades later for the Last Judgment on the altar wall, a darker, angrier work. Two rules inside: silence is enforced, and no photography — this is still a consecrated chapel, and it's where popes are elected.

St. Peter's Basilica

The largest church in Christendom, raised between 1506 and 1626 directly over the tomb of St. Peter himself — the fisherman-apostle crucified upside-down on this hill under Nero. Its greatest architects each left a hand: Bramante began it, Michelangelo designed the soaring dome, Maderno lengthened the nave, and Bernini gave the interior its drama. The scale plays tricks on you — the proportions are so harmonious it never feels as vast as it is, until you realize the marble cherubs holding the holy-water stoups are the size of grown men.

Your guide will lead the way, but the things not to miss: Michelangelo's Pietà (first chapel on the right), carved when he was just 24 — the impossibly tender Virgin cradling the dead Christ, and the only work he ever signed (across her sash, after he overheard the crowd credit it to a rival); it's behind glass now, after a 1972 hammer attack. At the crossing, Bernini's bronze Baldachin — a 29-meter canopy of twisting "Solomonic" columns — rises over the papal altar and the sunken confessio, set directly above St. Peter's tomb. Beyond it, in the apse, glows Bernini's Cathedra Petri (the Chair of St. Peter), bursting around a golden alabaster window with the dove of the Holy Spirit. Nearby, the bronze statue of St. Peter has one foot worn to a shine by centuries of pilgrims' hands. And in the Chapel of St. Sebastian — the second on the right, just past the Pietà — rests St. John Paul II, moved up from the crypt for his 2011 beatification and canonized in 2014, under a plain marble slab still visited by the faithful every day. One last thing to notice: there are no paintings in St. Peter's — every glowing "canvas" is actually a painstaking mosaic copy, made to endure the damp that would ruin paint. If your tour includes the dome climb or the Grottoes below (90+ papal tombs, including St. Peter's own), both are worth the extra time.

The Catacombs & Santa Maria Maggiore (Monday evening)

Miles of underground galleries where early Christians (and Jews, in separate catacombs) buried their dead, stacked in wall-niches called loculi, when Roman law forbade burial inside the city and Christians were a persecuted minority. They are not, despite the myth, where Christians mainly hid — but they are where the faith's earliest art survives, scratched and painted on the soft volcanic tufa: the fish, the anchor, the Good Shepherd, a whole vocabulary of code and hope. They run for kilometers on several levels along the Appian Way, the "queen of roads," whose original basalt paving stones still show the ruts of cartwheels. Cool, quiet, and genuinely moving.

Then the tour surfaces at Santa Maria Maggiore, one of Rome's four papal basilicas. Legend gives it the loveliest founding story in the city: the Virgin appeared in a dream and promised a sign, and on the morning of 5 August, in the dead of a Roman summer, snow fell on the Esquiline Hill and traced the outline of the church — which is why it's also called Our Lady of the Snow, and why every August 5th white petals shower down inside during Mass. The present building went up under Sixtus III (432–440), right after the Council of Ephesus proclaimed Mary Theotokos, Mother of God.

Look for: the 5th-century mosaics along the nave and triumphal arch — among the oldest and best-preserved Christian mosaics anywhere — and the 13th-century apse Coronation of the Virgin; the Salus Populi Romani, the ancient Marian icon (tradition credits St. Luke) carried through plague-stricken Rome in 590; and, down in the confessio beneath the papal altar, a crystal reliquary holding fragments venerated as the wood of the Holy Crib, brought by refugees fleeing the Holy Land — hence the church's other nickname, the Bethlehem of the West. Gian Lorenzo Bernini lies here too, under a floor slab so plain you could walk over it, beside his father Pietro. And since April 2025, so does Pope Francis — who prayed before that icon before and after every journey, broke a century of custom by choosing this basilica over the Vatican, and lies beneath a stone marked with a single word: Franciscus. Seeing it after hours, emptied of the day's pilgrims, is the point.

Trevi Fountain & the Spanish Steps

The Trevi is Baroque theater in stone: the sea-god Oceanus (everyone calls him Neptune) charges out of a palace wall on a shell-chariot drawn by sea-horses, at the display-end of the Acqua Vergine — an aqueduct built by Agrippa in 19 BC that still feeds the fountain today. Commissioned under Pope Clement XII and designed by Nicola Salvi (who won the competition in 1732), it was finished in 1762 with its central figures carved by Pietro Bracci — the largest Baroque fountain in Rome. The rite: back to the water, right hand over the left shoulder, toss one coin, and you're promised a return to Rome (the ~€3,000 tossed in daily is gathered for charity). It's pure cinema, too — this is where Anita Ekberg waded in Fellini's La Dolce Vita (1960). Go at dawn to have it nearly to yourselves.

The Spanish Steps — 135 of them, built 1723–25 to a design by Francesco de Sanctis — are the grandest staircase in Europe, sweeping up from Piazza di Spagna to the French church of Trinità dei Monti (the name comes from the Spanish embassy to the Holy See that has sat in the square for centuries). At the foot bubbles the Barcaccia, the half-sunk "leaky boat" fountain by Pietro Bernini (father of the more famous Gian Lorenzo). Just to the right, the Keats–Shelley House is where the poet John Keats died in 1821, only 25 years old. In spring the whole sweep is banked with pink azaleas — and it's a two-minute walk from where you're sleeping. (Since 2019 you're not allowed to sit on the steps — the police will move you along.)

FLORENCE — The Cradle of the Renaissance

Florence is small, walkable, and staggeringly concentrated: for about 150 years starting in the 1400s, this one merchant town — banking money, wool money, and the Medici family's patronage — produced a run of artists and thinkers that changed how the West sees the human figure, space, and itself. Almost everything you'll look at was made within a few streets of your apartment.

The Duomo & Brunelleschi's Dome (your climb)

The cathedral of Santa Maria del Fiore stood for decades with a hole in its roof, because no one knew how to build a dome that wide without the wooden centering scaffolds that didn't exist in a large enough form. A goldsmith with no formal architectural training, Filippo Brunelleschi, won the commission with a solution he refused to fully explain for fear of theft: a dome built as two shells, with the bricks laid in a self-supporting herringbone pattern that let each ring lock the last in place as it rose — no centering needed. Completed in 1436, it's still the largest masonry dome ever built. Your 463 steps climb between the two shells, which is why the stairs lean as you near the top. From the lantern, all of Tuscany opens up.

Beside it: Giotto's Campanile (the bell tower) and the Baptistery, whose gilded east doors Michelangelo is said to have called the "Gates of Paradise" — Ghiberti spent 27 years on them.

The Accademia — Michelangelo's David

You'll see David first thing, and early, which is the whole point — by mid-morning the hall is a wall of phones. Michelangelo carved it between 1501 and 1504 from a single flawed block of marble that two earlier sculptors had abandoned as ruined. He was 26. The figure is the moment before the fight — David watching Goliath approach, sling over one shoulder, weight shifting, brow furrowed — all tension, no triumph. It became the symbol of the Florentine Republic: the small free city ready to take on giants.

The corridor leading up to him holds the unfinished Prisoners (or Slaves), figures that seem to be struggling out of the raw stone. Many people find them more affecting than David himself — they're a window into how Michelangelo worked, believing the figure was already inside the marble and his job was only to free it.

The Uffizi

The Medici family's private collection, housed in what were the uffizi — the "offices" — of the Florentine state. It's a walk through the Renaissance in order. The highlights your guide will steer you to: Botticelli's Birth of Venus and Primavera, the great mythologies of the early Renaissance; the room of Leonardo, including an unfinished Adoration of the Magi that shows his method laid bare; and works by Michelangelo, Raphael, Titian, and Caravaggio. The rivalry to emphasize — the one that makes teenagers pay attention — is Leonardo vs. Michelangelo, two geniuses of the same city who couldn't stand each other. Look, too, for Artemisia Gentileschi's Judith Slaying Holofernes — painted by a woman who had prosecuted her own rapist in a Roman court, and who gave Judith the arms of someone who actually knew how to use a blade.

Chianti (your day in the hills)

South of Florence the Chianti hills roll out in cypress, vine, and stone — the classic Tuscan landscape. Chianti Classico is the historic heartland between Florence and Siena, and its bottles carry a black rooster (gallo nero) seal. The legend behind the bird: to settle their endless border war, Florence and Siena agreed each city would send a rider at cockcrow, and the frontier would be fixed where they met. The Sienese fattened a white rooster; the Florentines starved a black one, which crowed long before dawn — and their rider got most of the Chianti. The wine itself is Sangiovese, a grape that takes its character from altitude and stone rather than from oak. Everything you'll taste Thursday comes from within a few miles of Greve.

Impruneta & the terracotta (Fornace Masini). Your first stop is a kiln, not a winery — and it connects straight back to Tuesday night's climb. Impruneta sits on a seam of blue-grey clay so rich in iron that once fired it turns rose-red, rings like a bell when tapped, and shrugs off frost almost forever. Kilns have worked here since the Middle Ages (the potters formed a guild in 1308), and when Brunelleschi needed bricks and tiles strong enough to close his dome without supporting scaffolds, he came to Impruneta and — legend says — inspected them himself for flaws. That same cotto roofs the Duomo, paves the Medici villas, and forms the glazed robbiane of Luca della Robbia. The Masini family have worked here since 1934, in premises the Vanni kiln occupied back in 1681; the ancient wood-fired ovens still stand inside, now used as drying rooms. Your ~30-minute tour walks the full chain: raw clay, hand-pressing into century-old wooden moulds, slow drying, firing. Watch for the maker's mark — Impruneta kilns have signed and dated their pieces for generations. (One more Brunelleschi footnote: peposo, the black-pepper beef stew you'll see on menus all week, was supposedly cooked by the kilnmen in the mouths of their ovens.)

Florence American Cemetery. Before the hills, a quiet stop at Falciani: 4,400 American war dead from the 1944 campaign to push the Germans north up the Arno, in ranked marble crosses across a hillside. It takes fifteen minutes and lends the day its proportion.

Montefioralle. A walled hamlet of perhaps a hundred souls above Greve, one oval of stone lanes you can walk in ten minutes — the ancestral seat, it's claimed, of the Vespucci family, of whom Amerigo gave his name to two continents.

Lamole & I Fabbri. Lamole is the roof of Chianti Classico: a hilltop hamlet at ~600 m whose vineyards climb to 650 m — the ceiling for ripening Sangiovese in Tuscany. Its own designated growing zone since 2021, it's cooler and windier than the valleys, so the wines come out perfumed, high-toned, and taut rather than heavy. What makes it visually astonishing is the terracing: miles of dry-stone walls, stacked by hand over centuries, that hold the soil against the slope and bounce sunlight back onto the vines by day, releasing stored heat at night. I Fabbri is Susanna Grassi's small certified-organic estate (~12 hectares) — she left a city career to rebuild her family's terraces, restoring them in 2002 — making minimal-intervention Sangiovese aged in big Slavonian oak casks. It's the human scale of this place that lands: the winemaker herself, a natural amphitheater of walls, and the whole of Chianti falling away below.

Vecchie Terre di Montefili. In the afternoon, a hilltop between Greve and Panzano at over 500 m, with vines planted as far back as 1975. Led by winemaker-agronomist Serena Gusmeri, they farm micro-parcels separately and vinify each in small lots, chasing purity over power — cool nights and rocky soil give old-vine Sangiovese with high acidity and long, slow ripening. Ask about Anfiteatro (their old-vine Sangiovese) and Bruno di Rocca. Wine Enthusiast has scored their Chianti Classico in the mid-90s. The road up is narrow, winding, and part gravel — hence Simone at the wheel.

A note on the boys: tasting rooms here are gracious about teenagers; a small pour from a parent's glass is normal and unremarked. The wine is the excuse — the day is really about a landscape that has been farmed, walled, and fired into being by hand.

Your Pasta Class at Cuculia (Wednesday evening)

Cuculia sits on Via dei Serragli in the Oltrarno, the artisans' quarter across the river — a restaurant with a bookshop's soul, named for the cuckoo. For three hours (4:30–7:30) you'll work in its kitchen with the Pasta Lovers team, then eat what you made, with a bottle of organic Chianti on the table. Expect to learn the two great families of fresh pasta: an egg dough (flour + yolks, kneaded and rested, rolled thin for tagliatelle or ravioli) and a water-and-flour dough — the peasant kind that needs no eggs, hand-rolled into the fat, chewy pici of southern Tuscany. The technique worth stealing is the finish: pasta is never sauced on the plate but tossed in the pan with a ladle of its starchy cooking water, which emulsifies the fat into a sauce that clings. Bring an appetite and expect flour on your shirt. Afterwards it's a lovely walk home over the Ponte Vecchio.

Ponte Vecchio & the Oltrarno

The Ponte Vecchio ("old bridge") is the only Florence bridge the retreating Germans didn't blow up in 1944, reportedly on Hitler's personal order. Its shops have sold gold since the 1590s, when the Medici evicted the butchers and tanners (whose smell offended the private corridor the family used to cross above the shops). Across the river, the Oltrarno is the artisans' quarter — leather, gilding, paper marbling — and the least touristy side of the center.

VENICE — La Serenissima

Venice is a city built on wooden pilings driven into the mud of a lagoon, which for a thousand years was an independent republic — La Serenissima, "the Most Serene" — and one of the richest states in the world, the hinge of trade between Europe and the East. It has no cars, no ground streets in the usual sense, and a logic all its own: you navigate by water and by walking, and getting lost is part of the design. Everything you'll see was paid for by maritime empire.

St. Mark's Basilica (your night tour)

Not a cathedral of stone so much as a cavern of gold. Above you, roughly 8,000 square meters of Byzantine mosaic on gold-glass ground — the largest and finest such ceiling in the Western world, glinting differently as you move. The basilica was built to house the body of St. Mark the Evangelist, which Venetian merchants stole from Alexandria in 828 AD, smuggling it past Muslim inspectors (so the story goes) hidden under pork. Much of the marble, columns, and treasure was likewise carried home as plunder — most spectacularly the four bronze horses looted from Constantinople in 1204 (the ones outside are copies; the originals are inside). Behind the altar, the Pala d'Oro is a screen of some 2,000 gems and enamels. Seeing all this lit and near-empty at night is a completely different experience from the daytime crush — a very good decision.

The Doge's Palace & Secret Itineraries

For a millennium this was the seat of Venetian power — the residence of the elected Doge, the council chambers, the courts, and (over an internal bridge) the prisons. The state was a tightly controlled republic run by its patrician families, watchful and secretive, and the Secret Itineraries tour takes you into the machinery that daytime visitors never see: the wood-lined offices, the interrogation and torture rooms, the Piombi ("the Leads") — the attic cells under the lead roof — and the route by which Casanova, imprisoned there in 1755, became the only person ever to escape, breaking up through the ceiling and walking out the front door dressed as a gentleman.

The Bridge of Sighs connected the palace to the newer prison; the romantic name is a 19th-century invention — the "sighs" were supposedly those of prisoners glimpsing their last view of freedom through its windows.

Rialto & the Bacari

The Rialto was the commercial and financial heart of the republic — its bridge is the oldest across the Grand Canal, and its fish and produce markets have run in the same spot for roughly a thousand years. Go early; by mid-morning it's done. Around it survive the bacari — Venice's old wine bars, where the tradition is cicchetti (small bites, like Venetian tapas) and an ombra (a small glass of wine). This is how Venetians actually eat and drink, standing up, in between things. Dishes to try: baccalà mantecato (whipped salt cod) and sarde in saor (sweet-and-sour sardines, a sailors' preserving recipe 600 years old).

Murano & the Glass (your private demonstration)

Venice moved its glassmakers to the island of Murano in 1291 — officially to protect the city from furnace fires, in practice to guard the secrets of a fantastically valuable industry. Master glassblowers were forbidden to leave the republic on pain of death; the techniques they perfected — cristallo, filigree, millefiori — made Murano glass the standard of luxury across Europe for centuries. Watching a master pull a shape out of molten glass in ninety seconds is one of those things that can't be reproduced anywhere else, and a private demonstration means no crowd between you and the furnace.

The Gondola

The gondola is not a tourist gimmick but the vessel Venice was built around — asymmetrical by design (the left side is wider, to offset the single oarsman's stroke), once numbered in the thousands, now a preserved craft. At golden hour, through the quiet back canals of Dorsoduro and San Polo rather than the crowded basin, it's the correct way to read the city's water-geography: how the palaces face the canals because the canals were the roads.

Buon viaggio. Everything above is here to be used lightly — the point of the whole trip is standing in front of these things together. The rest is just so you'll know what you're looking at.
